Distributed Consensus: From Paxos to Raft

Introduction

A distributed system is one in which the failure of a computer you didn't even know existed can render your own computer unusable. That observation, attributed to Leslie Lamport, captures the central difficulty: when state is split across machines that fail independently and communicate over a network that may delay, reorder, or drop messages, the simple operations we take for granted on a single machine — "store this value", "read the latest value" — become surprisingly hard. Distributed consensus is the formalism we use to make them possible again. A consensus protocol lets a set of machines agree on a single value, even when some machines crash and the network misbehaves, with the guarantee that once agreement is reached it cannot be undone. Every replicated database, every leader election in a cluster manager, every blockchain block confirmation rides on top of a consensus protocol whether the authors realize it or not.

The FLP Impossibility and Why It Doesn't Stop Us

In 1985, Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson proved that in a fully asynchronous system, no deterministic consensus protocol can guarantee progress in the presence of even a single faulty process. This result is often misunderstood as saying consensus is impossible. It is not. It says you cannot have all three of: safety (no two correct processes decide on different values), liveness (every correct process eventually decides), and tolerance of asynchrony with even one failure. Real protocols pick two and finesse the third. Paxos and Raft both guarantee safety unconditionally and provide liveness when the network is "eventually synchronous" — that is, messages are delivered within some unknown but bounded time after some point in time. In practice this means progress halts during severe network partitions but resumes once the network heals, and crucially, no incorrect decision is ever made. This is the right trade for systems where a wrong answer is worse than no answer.

Paxos: The Original Insight

Paxos, introduced by Leslie Lamport in 1989 and published in 1998, was the first consensus protocol that both worked correctly and was efficient enough to deploy. It proceeds in two phases. In the prepare phase, a proposer picks a proposal number larger than any it has seen and asks a majority of acceptors whether they have promised to ignore proposals with smaller numbers. If a majority promise, the proposer learns the highest-numbered accepted value among the responses, if any. In the accept phase, the proposer asks the majority to accept a value: either the value it learned in phase one, or, if no value was learned, the value it originally wanted to propose. An acceptor accepts the proposal as long as it has not since promised to ignore that proposal number.

The genius of Paxos is in what it does not require. It does not require a leader. It does not require synchronized clocks. It does not require ordered message delivery. It does not even require that the same proposer drive both phases — a different proposer can pick up mid-way. The protocol's safety comes from a simple invariant: any value chosen must be supported by a quorum, and any two quorums intersect in at least one acceptor, so any subsequent proposal will learn about the chosen value through that overlap and propagate it. This is why majority quorums are non-negotiable. In a five-node cluster, three is the magic number for both phases. With four, you can read but not write safely; with two, you can be partitioned away into a minority that thinks it is the majority. The math is unforgiving and elegant in equal measure.

Multi-Paxos and the Hidden Complexity

Single-decree Paxos decides on one value. To run a state machine — say, a replicated key-value store — you need a sequence of decisions, one per command. Multi-Paxos optimizes this by electing a stable leader who skips the prepare phase for subsequent decisions, reducing the per-command cost to a single round trip. This sounds simple. It is not. The original Paxos paper deliberately left these details vague, and a generation of engineers learned the hard way that getting log compaction right, handling leader changes safely, and dealing with reconfiguration of the membership set are each their own subtle puzzles. Production Paxos implementations like Google's Chubby and Spanner contain thousands of lines of code where the original Paxos paper has perhaps a hundred. Most of that code handles cases the paper did not address explicitly: what if a leader fails mid-batch, what if two leaders briefly coexist during a network partition that heals, what if a new follower joins and needs to catch up on a million committed log entries.

The result was a kind of folk wisdom in the distributed systems community: Paxos is correct, but nobody can implement it the same way twice. This was the soil in which Raft grew.

Raft: Designing for Understandability

Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout published Raft in 2014 with an explicit non-functional goal: understandability. They argued that the difficulty of teaching, implementing, and reasoning about Paxos was itself a correctness liability — bugs lurked in the gap between what implementers thought the protocol said and what it actually required. Raft achieves equivalent guarantees to Multi-Paxos but reorganizes the problem around three sub-problems that can be reasoned about more independently: leader election, log replication, and safety.

In Raft, time is divided into terms, each of which begins with an election. Each server starts as a follower. If a follower receives no heartbeat from a leader within an election timeout, it becomes a candidate, increments its term, votes for itself, and asks others for their votes. A server grants its vote in a given term to at most one candidate, on a first-come-first-served basis, but only if the candidate's log is at least as up-to-date as its own. A candidate that gathers votes from a majority of the cluster becomes the leader for that term. The leader then sends periodic heartbeats to suppress new elections and replicates client commands to the followers' logs.

The log replication step is where Raft makes its biggest deviation from Paxos in spirit: the leader maintains the invariant that the leader's log is always the truth, and followers passively accept whatever the leader sends. A new command is appended to the leader's log first, then replicated to followers via AppendEntries RPCs. Once a majority of servers have stored the entry, it is considered committed and can be applied to the state machine. The next AppendEntries RPC informs followers of the new commit index, and they apply the entry to their own state machines in order.

Safety in Raft rests on a property called the Log Matching Property: if two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, then the logs are identical in all entries up through that index. This is preserved because the leader never overwrites entries in its own log, and followers' inconsistent suffixes are pruned by the leader's AppendEntries consistency check. Combined with the election restriction that a candidate must have a log at least as up-to-date as a majority before it can win, this guarantees that any committed entry is present in the log of every future leader.

The Real Tradeoffs

For most engineers, the choice between Paxos and Raft is less about correctness and more about ergonomics. Both are safe. Both are live under eventual synchrony. The practical differences are these. Raft assumes a strong leader, which simplifies reasoning but means leader election cost is paid every time the leader fails or a network partition isolates it. Paxos, in its multi-decree form, can also use a leader but does not require one — proposers can compete without violating safety, at the cost of contention and wasted work. Raft's log is more rigidly structured, which makes it easier to reason about and to implement correctly, but harder to optimize for specialized workloads. Paxos is more flexible: variants like Flexible Paxos relax the quorum requirements when you can guarantee certain network properties, enabling lower latency in geo-distributed deployments.

Latency tells a similar story. In the common case where a leader is stable and the network is healthy, both protocols require one round trip from leader to a majority of followers for each command. That is the irreducible cost: you cannot commit a value until you know a majority has stored it. Variants like batching, pipelining, and using leases can amortize or hide this cost but cannot eliminate it. Bandwidth requirements are similar for both protocols and grow linearly with the size of the log entries, which is why log compaction (via snapshotting) is a load-bearing optimization in any production deployment. A consensus log that grows unboundedly will eventually exhaust disk and slow replication; periodic snapshots replace the log prefix with a compact state representation that new followers can load in bulk.

Common Misunderstandings

A few traps catch even experienced engineers. First, consensus does not solve the split-brain problem on its own — it solves the in-cluster split-brain, but client-side coordination is still needed to avoid two clients believing they are talking to the canonical leader. Leases and fencing tokens are the usual remedies, and forgetting them has caused real outages at major companies. Second, the f+1 formula for fault tolerance refers to crash failures, not Byzantine failures (arbitrary, possibly malicious behavior). To tolerate f Byzantine failures you need 3f+1 nodes, not 2f+1, and protocols like PBFT or HotStuff rather than Paxos or Raft. Most production systems assume crash-only failures because Byzantine tolerance is expensive and the threat model usually doesn't justify it. Third, "strong consistency" in the consensus sense is about agreement on the sequence of operations, not about real-time freshness; a reader that bypasses the consensus log can read stale data even in a system using Raft. Production systems handle this with read leases or by routing all reads through the leader.

Where This Lands in Practice

Most modern infrastructure consensus is Raft. etcd, Consul, TiKV, CockroachDB, and many newer databases use Raft variants. Apache Zookeeper uses Zab, a Paxos-inspired protocol with a strong leader and a specific recovery procedure. Spanner uses Multi-Paxos under the hood. Blockchain protocols like Tendermint and HotStuff are descended from PBFT rather than Paxos but solve the same underlying problem with Byzantine tolerance. The high-level point is that the protocol you pick matters less than understanding what guarantees you are relying on and what failure modes those guarantees allow. A team that picks Raft because they read the paper and understood it tends to ship a more reliable system than a team that picked Paxos because it was the prestige choice and then implemented half of it.

The lesson distributed systems engineers eventually internalize is this: consensus is the floor, not the ceiling. It guarantees agreement on what happened. It does not guarantee that what you agreed on was the right thing, that the application logic above it is correct, that your operators won't accidentally configure a minority partition as authoritative, or that your monitoring will catch a failed follower before its log diverges too far to catch up. The protocol is necessary. It is rarely sufficient.
